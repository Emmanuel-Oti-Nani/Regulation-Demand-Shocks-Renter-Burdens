Interactive results · U.S. metropolitan areas, 2013–2024

Did land-use regulation make demand shocks hit renters harder?

About half of U.S. renter households spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing. This site lets you explore renter cost burdens, rents, permits and regulation across 367 metropolitan areas, and see the study's main estimates.

Emmanuel Oti Nani · ORCID Data and code on GitHub

Key findings

Employment growth lowered burdens. Metros with faster predicted job growth saw renter cost burdens fall, especially for renters earning under $35,000.
Remote work raised them. Metros where more people already worked from home in 2019 saw rents and burdens rise after 2020, mostly among middle- and higher-income renters.
Regulation did not amplify either shock. Neither effect was stronger in more restrictive metros, and permitting did not respond less there. The estimates are precise enough to rule out large amplification.

The main estimates

Each dot is how much more (or less) a demand shock moved renter cost burdens in a metro one standard deviation more restrictive on the Wharton index. Positive values would mean regulation amplified the shock. Thick bars are 90% confidence intervals and thin bars 95%. Units are percentage points of renters.
